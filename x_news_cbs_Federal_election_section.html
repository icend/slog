原文

Federal actions around voting system expand weeks ahead of midterms. With midterm elections just two weeks away, there are concerns that the federal government's pressure on election officials is breeding distrust in the system. Federal agents have seized ballots, and voting machines have been examined. The Justice Department is locked in legal battles with states across the country over information about individual voters and this past week threatened to prosecute California election officials if they knowingly count mail-in ballots from 20,000 people the Trump administration says are "likely noncitizens." It's a kind of interference Utah Lt. Gov. Deidre Henderson, a Republican in a deeply red state, said she's never seen before.
"In the past two elections that I oversaw as lieutenant governor, the threats were all external: non-state actors, cyber security threats and other things," she said. "But now we also have threats coming from within our own government." Federal government actions on elections. In the U.S., elections are local. They're run by towns and counties and states. It's stated in Article I of the Constitution. Congress makes laws regulating elections, but the president doesn't have a role in running them. Still, during his second term Mr. Trump has signed two executive orders around voting: one requiring documentary proof of citizenship to register to vote and another on mail-in ballots. The administration's latest move came Thursday, when the Justice Department sued California, claiming it was mailing ballots to more than 20,000 likely noncitizens. California Secretary of State Shirley Weber said the federal government was threatening the right of thousands of Californians to vote without providing any detail about how they identified those individuals, and she questioned the accuracy of the data. The Justice Department's "relentless efforts to unlawfully gain access to states' protected voter data and purge voter rolls serve only to cause chaos and fuel public distrust in our elections," Weber said in a statement. "Noncitizens cannot vote in federal elections," head of the Justice Department's Civil Rights Division Harmeet Dhillon told 60 Minutes. "So the question is simply how many of them there are."
